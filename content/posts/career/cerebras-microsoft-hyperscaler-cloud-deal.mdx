## What Was Actually Agreed

On November 12, [Cerebras Systems](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002021728/000162828026073366/cbrsannouncesfinancialresu.htm) disclosed on its Q3 2026 earnings call that Microsoft has signed a cloud computing agreement with the company, making it Cerebras's third hyperscaler partner after Amazon Web Services and Oracle. CEO Andrew Feldman told [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/12/cerebras-systems-cbrs-q3-earnings-report-2026.html) the agreement was actually signed in October and that Cerebras expects revenue from it to begin in the first quarter of 2027, when the companies plan to share more detail. Beyond calling it a cloud inference agreement, neither company has disclosed pricing, capacity, or what Azure customers will be able to buy because of it.

The pattern from the other two deals suggests the shape this one might take: AWS pairs its own Trainium3 chips with Cerebras hardware to split inference work between them, while Oracle is installing Cerebras systems directly inside its own data centers to resell as an Oracle Cloud Infrastructure product. Whether Microsoft's deal copies either model, or does something else entirely, is exactly what hasn't been disclosed.

Cerebras's wafer-scale chips are built for inference speed, not training, and as AI labs push real-time agents and reasoning models that need fast answers, inference capacity has become one of the most contested parts of the AI stack. Cerebras has spent 2026 racing to get its hardware installed inside every major cloud before Nvidia's own inference offerings close the speed gap it's built its business on. The OpenAI deal from January, worth more than $10 billion for 750 megawatts of compute, is reportedly the bulk of the $26.1 billion in contracted-but-unbilled revenue Cerebras disclosed this quarter. Landing Microsoft on top of AWS and Oracle means all three Western hyperscalers have now put some chips behind Cerebras's architecture, at a moment when Feldman told investors the industry's binding constraint, in his words, is "rapid change and compute shortages."

## What Each Side Gets, and Who It Affects

Cerebras gets distribution inside Azure, the one major hyperscaler it hadn't landed, plus the credibility of being chosen by all three dominant Western clouds rather than just one. Microsoft gets an inference option beyond Nvidia GPUs and its own Maia chips, at a time when any additional AI-serving capacity is scarce enough to matter on its own. What Microsoft gives up, or pays, is undisclosed, and so is whether Azure customers get direct access to Cerebras hardware or whether it's folded into existing Microsoft AI products instead. Nvidia is the party with the most reason to watch closely: every hyperscaler that diversifies its inference stack is one more customer with a credible alternative supplier. AMD, which separately partners with Cerebras on a rack-scale inference platform, and Groq, Cerebras's closest direct rival in specialized inference chips, both have reason to track whether this pulls more hyperscaler spending toward alternative silicon generally, rather than toward either of them specifically.

## What to Watch Next

Three things would turn this from a one-line disclosure on an earnings call into confirmation of a real partnership: whether Cerebras and Microsoft ever issue a joint announcement describing the deal, the way AWS and Oracle did at the time; whether revenue actually shows up in Cerebras's results in the first quarter of 2027, on the schedule Feldman gave; and whether it arrives bundled with an actual Azure product that Microsoft's customers can buy, rather than compute Microsoft keeps for itself.
