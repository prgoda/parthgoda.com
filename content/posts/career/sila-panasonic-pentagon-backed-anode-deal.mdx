## What Was Actually Agreed

On November 18, [Sila](https://www.financialcontent.com/article/bizwire-2026-11-18-sila-inks-long-term-offtake-deal-with-panasonic-energy-to-power-domestic-battery-production) signed a multi-year commercial offtake agreement with Panasonic Energy Corporation of North America, committing Panasonic to buy Sila's Titan Silicon silicon-carbon anode material from Sila's plant in Moses Lake, Washington. Sila says the agreement is part of a long-term relationship with the potential to exceed $1 billion in offtake over time — but neither company disclosed tonnage or price; [MIT Technology Review reports](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/11/19/1148922/sila-panasonic-battery-deal/) Panasonic has committed to buying the material in bulk, but that Sila isn't sharing the specific volume or what Panasonic is paying for it. The material goes into battery cells Panasonic builds at its U.S. factories. The two companies also agreed to collaborate on intellectual property tied to the anode, without detailing what that covers. Supply isn't immediate — Panasonic still has to qualify Sila's material at volume before shipments begin — and Sila says the scale-up needed to meet the deal will add roughly 500 manufacturing and construction jobs at Moses Lake, per [Columbia Basin Herald's coverage](https://columbiabasinherald.com/news/2026/nov/19/sila-announces-deal-with-panasonic-energy-corporation/).

## Why Now

The timeline above is the real story: this isn't a cold-start partnership, it's a three-year relationship getting converted into a firm commitment the moment Sila could actually deliver at scale. Panasonic first ordered Sila's material back in December 2023, when Sila's Moses Lake plant was still under construction. What changed between then and now is capital and capacity. In July 2026, Sila raised $300 million in private funding; the next month, the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital offered Sila a conditional loan of up to $1.4 billion specifically to expand Moses Lake's silicon-carbon output and fund a lithium-ion cell plant, money tied to [reducing U.S. dependence on foreign battery-material supply chains](https://www.electrive.com/2026/08/11/us-government-offers-sila-up-to-1-4-billion-in-funding/), where China still dominates graphite mining and anode processing. Reading the November deal as Panasonic finally committing real volume once Sila had both the federal backing and the commissioned plant to execute is an interpretation, not something either company stated, but the three-month gap between the loan and the offtake supports it.

Neither side gives up much. Panasonic isn't exclusive to Sila — it also sources silicon-anode material from the British startup Nexeon — and Sila keeps supplying other customers, including Mercedes-Benz's electric G-Class program. Neither side has proof the arrangement scales past a press release: the "over $1 billion" figure is Sila's own characterization of potential volume, not a guaranteed contract value, and the Pentagon loan remains conditional on terms that haven't closed.

## Who It Affects, and What to Watch

The deal sharpens a contrast with Group14 Technologies, the other major U.S. silicon-anode startup, which has leaned on a South Korea factory alongside its U.S. sites rather than an all-domestic buildout — a distinction federal industrial-policy dollars may end up rewarding. It also adds pressure on Chinese graphite suppliers, as cell makers hedge toward silicon-carbon blends partly to diversify geography, not just chemistry. Three things worth watching: whether Panasonic qualifies Moses Lake's material at the volumes this deal implies; whether the Pentagon loan converts from conditional to signed; and whether Sila lands a second major cell-maker customer beyond Panasonic and Mercedes — the real test of whether Moses Lake is a platform business or a two-customer bet.
