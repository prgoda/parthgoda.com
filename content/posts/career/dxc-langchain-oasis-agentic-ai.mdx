## What Was Actually Agreed

On October 13, [DXC Technology and LangChain announced they are advancing their partnership](https://dxc.com/newsroom/10132026-dxc-and-langchain-advance-agentic-ai-harnesses) around DXC OASIS, the agentic IT-operations platform DXC introduced in April. OASIS already ran on LangChain's open-source LangGraph for agent orchestration; DXC is now also licensing LangSmith, LangChain's paid tool for building, evaluating, and monitoring agents in production, to run quality control on what OASIS's agents do. Neither company disclosed the license's price, duration, or whether the arrangement is exclusive.

OASIS is live with real clients and, per DXC, scaling toward 125 customer environments across regulated industries and multiple geographies. DXC's own figures — not independently verified by either [IT Brief](https://itbrief.com.au/story/dxc-partners-langchain-to-govern-enterprise-ai-agents) or any other outlet covering the announcement — claim a 95% average cut in resolution time and 92%-or-better diagnostic accuracy against senior-engineer judgment, measured across more than 500 use cases. DXC says the system pauses for human sign-off before taking any action it judges hard to reverse.

## What Each Side Gets

DXC gets a working agent-orchestration and evaluation layer it didn't have to build, letting it brand and sell OASIS as proprietary IT-ops automation while the actual reasoning engine underneath belongs to a startup it doesn't own. What DXC gives up is harder to see today, since nothing public ties LangChain to DXC specifically — any competitor can license the same tools. LangChain gets a marquee reference account: a global services firm running its stack inside "mission-critical" systems at regulated enterprises, the kind of validation an infrastructure startup needs as it competes with Microsoft, Google, and Salesforce for the same layer of the stack.

The timing tracks a leadership change as much as a product milestone. DXC launched OASIS on April 28 under then-infrastructure president Chris Drumgoole. On July 30, [Dan Gray — OASIS's own architect as the unit's CTO — was promoted to run it](https://dxc.com/newsroom/07302026-dxc-announces-leadership-appointments-to-accelerate-its-ai-centric-future). Licensing LangSmith followed less than three months later, with the executive who built OASIS now owning the case for widening it. Reading the expanded deal as evidence the agentic bet is now core strategy, not a project under new management, is an interpretation DXC didn't state outright — but the sequence supports it. DXC isn't alone in moving: [Infosys partnered with Anthropic](https://itbrief.com.au/story/dxc-partners-langchain-to-govern-enterprise-ai-agents) earlier this year to chase the same regulated-industry opening, and Accenture sells its own proprietary AI Refinery platform rather than licensing one. Standing still while rivals define how agentic AI enters managed IT services was the less attractive option.

## Who It Affects

DXC's enterprise clients, many in regulated sectors, now have agents built on a third party's framework touching finance, infrastructure, and operations systems — worth a compliance team's attention even with the human-approval checkpoints DXC describes. Accenture, Infosys, Cognizant, and other IT-services rivals face the same build-or-license choice DXC just made, and DXC is betting on licensing. LangChain's infrastructure competitors — Microsoft's agent tooling, Google's Agent Development Kit, Salesforce's Agentforce — now have a concrete example of a hyperscale services firm choosing LangChain's stack over building its own.

## What to Watch Next

Three signals would show this is working rather than merely renewed: whether DXC or any of its target clients lets an independent party verify the 95%/92% performance figures; whether DXC ever diversifies OASIS's orchestration layer beyond LangChain, the kind of multi-sourcing enterprises typically want to avoid lock-in; and whether LangChain lands a similarly sized deal with a DXC competitor, which would suggest it's becoming the default agent-infrastructure layer for IT services rather than a bet specific to DXC.
