## What Was Actually Agreed

On November 4, [Qualcomm announced](https://x.com/Qualcomm/status/2117972701197598803) that SpaceX selected its Dragonwing Cellular Infrastructure platforms to build the ground-based 5G network for Starlink Mobile, SpaceX's planned push into terrestrial wireless service in the US. Under the multi-year agreement, [Reuters reported](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/qualcomm-signs-major-5g-infrastructure-deal-with-spacex-for-starlink-mobile-network-4999443), Qualcomm supplies the radio units, baseband units (its QRU100 and QDU100 chips), and Layer 1 software for Massive MIMO base stations — using 64 transmit and 64 receive paths per radio — at tens of thousands of U.S. cell sites. Deployment is slated to start in 2027. Neither company disclosed the deal's financial terms. Qualcomm is calling it [its largest 5G infrastructure win to date](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/04/spacex-strikes-deal-with-qualcomm-for-mobile-network-infrastructure.html).

The sequence matters more than the deal itself. SpaceX's only mobile product until now was Direct to Cell, a satellite service that piggybacks on T-Mobile's licensed spectrum, which [went into public beta in 2025](https://www.androidauthority.com/starlink-satellite-t-mobile-3477849/) — a vendor role, not a carrier role. That changed in May 2026, when the FCC [approved](https://www.advanced-television.com/2026/05/13/fcc-approves-echostars-spectrum-sale-to-spacex-att/) SpaceX's roughly $17 billion purchase of its own AWS-3, AWS-4, and H-block spectrum from EchoStar (AT&T bought the rest in the same split). Around the same time, [the FT reported, via Reuters](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/06/26/musk039s-spacex-targets-us-consumers-with-starlink-mobile-service-push-ft-reports), that president Gwynne Shotwell told investors SpaceX might build its own terrestrial network, and [Bloomberg reported](https://www.stockopedia.com/share-prices/t-mobile-us-NSQ:TMUS/news/space-x-charter-discuss-mobile-phone-partnership-in-us-bloomberg-news-says-updated-019f0652-a91a-79c6-bbc5-2ab2ccbbe0c0/) talks with Charter Communications on a phone partnership. Spectrum without ground equipment is unusable; Qualcomm supplies the other half of what a carrier needs.

## What Each Side Gets

SpaceX gets a supplier with a two-decade track record building carrier-grade radios and basebands, skipping years it would otherwise spend developing cellular silicon itself. Qualcomm gets [its largest 5G infrastructure contract to date](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/04/spacex-strikes-deal-with-qualcomm-for-mobile-network-infrastructure.html), won over rival RAN-chip suppliers Marvell, Broadcom, and Intel, and a foothold in what could be among the largest greenfield 5G buildouts in the US this decade. What SpaceX gives up is optionality: tying the network to one chip architecture makes switching vendors expensive later, the lock-in every carrier already accepts when it picks a RAN supplier.

## Who It Affects

AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile are the direct targets — SpaceX has described the ambition as challenging the three incumbents head-on, which puts T-Mobile in the odd position of competing with a company it also depends on for its Direct to Cell satellite feature. Marvell, Broadcom, and Intel lost a contract in a RAN-chip market that doesn't have many deals this size to go around. And EchoStar, having sold its spectrum rather than build out the network itself, now watches both buyers — SpaceX and AT&T — move into the market it couldn't finish.

## What to Watch Next

Whether SpaceX names a specific launch date, price, or coverage map for Starlink Mobile — as of this deal, none of the three existed. Whether the 2027 construction timeline holds, given how often large US tower buildouts slip. And whether T-Mobile's existing satellite relationship with SpaceX survives the two companies becoming terrestrial competitors, or gets renegotiated once Starlink Mobile has its own ground network to fall back on.
