## What Was Actually Agreed

On November 19, [Google announced](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/blue-origin-project-suncatcher-partnership/) a launch services agreement with Blue Origin: the first dedicated, heavy-lift mission for Project Suncatcher, Google's effort to run AI compute on solar-powered satellites in orbit. Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket will carry the payload into sun-synchronous orbit, targeted for the first quarter of 2029. Each satellite is built around Google's own TPU chips and sized to roughly one data-center rack's worth of compute, according to [Satnews](https://satnews.com/2026/11/19/google-selects-blue-origins-new-glenn-rocket-for-2029-ai-satellite-launch/) and [Via Satellite](https://www.satellitetoday.com/launch/2026/11/19/google-orders-heavy-lift-launch-for-project-suncatcher-from-blue-origin/). Google's Travis Beals, senior director of Project Suncatcher, called it "a crucial milestone on our roadmap as we expand the boundaries of what's possible for the future of compute." Neither company disclosed what Google is paying, how many satellites will fly, or whether further New Glenn launches are planned.

The prototype worked well enough to justify committing to a dedicated mission: Google's October 1 satellite, built with Planet Labs and launched on a SpaceX rocket, successfully ran initial machine-learning tests between TPU chips in orbit. Google's next step before the 2029 launch is a 2027 test pairing two satellites over a laser link, the harder problem its [own research paper](https://research.google/blog/exploring-a-space-based-scalable-ai-infrastructure-system-design/) flags as unproven at scale. Reading the New Glenn booking as Google deliberately diversifying away from SpaceX — rather than simply picking whichever rocket was available in 2029 — is an interpretation neither company has confirmed, but the timing is suggestive: Blue Origin's heavy-lift rocket has flown commercially once, in April, on a mission that failed, and then saw its only operational heavy-lift pad damaged in a May 28 explosion. Google booked it anyway.

## What Each Side Gets, and Who It Affects

Blue Origin gets a marquee customer in the AI infrastructure race at a moment it badly needs one: New Glenn's commercial record is one failed flight and one launchpad explosion, and a Google booking is a vote of confidence other customers can point to. It joins Amazon's Kuiper constellation, AST SpaceMobile, NASA's Artemis lander program, and a Space Force contract on Blue Origin's manifest. Google gets a second launch provider for a program it has staked real research investment in, and some insulation from relying solely on SpaceX — which is not a neutral vendor here. Elon Musk has said SpaceX intends to put data centers in space itself, scaling up next-generation Starlink satellites for the job, and has targeted deployments as early as 2028. Every dollar Google sends to Blue Origin instead is a dollar it isn't sending to a direct competitor in the same emerging market. Starcloud, recently valued near $1.1 billion, and Axiom Space, which launched its first orbital data-center nodes in January, are both chasing the same niche and now watch two better-funded rivals pick sides.

## What to Watch Next

Three things separate this from a press release: whether New Glenn returns to flight cleanly before the end of 2026, as Blue Origin has said it expects; whether the 2027 two-satellite laser-link test actually demonstrates the intersatellite communication Google's own paper says is unresolved; and whether Google orders more than this one New Glenn launch, which would confirm this is a real hedge against SpaceX rather than a one-off booking made before 2029 options narrowed.
