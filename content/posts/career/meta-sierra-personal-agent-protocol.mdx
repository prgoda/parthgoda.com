## What Was Actually Agreed

On October 6, Sierra, the enterprise AI agent company co-founded by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor, and Meta announced they are co-developing the [Personal Agent Protocol](https://sierra.ai/blog/introducing-personal-agent-protocol) (PAP), an open, OAuth-based standard for how personal AI agents authenticate and act on a customer's behalf when dealing with a business. Genesys, Shopify, Stripe, Walmart, and two less widely known names, Instinct and Rocket, are named as partners alongside Sierra and Meta, according to both [Sierra's announcement](https://sierra.ai/blog/introducing-personal-agent-protocol) and [independent reporting from PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/meta-and-sierra-build-standard-for-how-personal-agents-interact-with-businesses/).

Stripped of the launch language, the protocol does three specific things. It lets a customer grant their personal agent guest access, a signed-in session, or standing credentials to a company's systems, with the customer choosing whether the agent gets read-only or read/write access. It carries that permission across channels, so a question an agent asks before login and an order it places after login count as one continuous, authenticated session rather than two unrelated visits. And it gives the business a consistent way to see what an agent is doing on its site or through its API, instead of having to guess whether a given visitor is a person or a bot. A version 0.1 specification, along with a reference implementation, is due later in October, per [Sierra's post](https://sierra.ai/blog/introducing-personal-agent-protocol).

## What Each Side Gets, and Gives Up

For Sierra, a roughly four-year-old startup, co-authoring the access standard for agentic commerce is a significant positioning win: it moves the company from "a vendor of customer-service agents" toward "the company that wrote the plumbing everyone else's agents run on." For Meta, the protocol gives its Muse personal agent a sanctioned, consent-based way to transact with retailers, something it currently lacks. For Walmart, Shopify, Stripe, and Genesys, participating means having a say in the terms under which outside agents touch their systems, rather than having those terms set unilaterally by whichever AI lab's agent shows up next.

None of the companies described any exclusivity, revenue share, or licensing terms attached to the protocol. Taylor told [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/06/meta-teams-up-with-bret-taylors-sierra-technologies-on-new-standards-for-ai-agent-commerce/) it is "open for anyone to implement," which means no partner is buying exclusive access or lock-in through this deal. What is not yet disclosed, and what a [critical analysis from Rajesh Beri](https://www.beri.net/article/meta-sierra-personal-agent-protocol-oauth-guest-read-write-access-vs-ucp-acp-trusted-agent-protocol-retail-banks) flags directly, is who governs the standard once it exists: Sierra and Meta wrote the first draft, and it is an open question whether a wider body will control it later, or whether, in Beri's framing, "whoever writes the lock decides who walks through it."

## Why Now

The timing traces back three weeks. On September 21, Amazon blocked Meta's Muse agent from its retail site, alleging Muse accessed the store without disclosing it was automated and without authorization, and that it captured customer login credentials in the process, according to [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/amazon-blocks-meta-s-muse-ai-agent-from-its-retail-site). Amazon has also blocked agents from OpenAI, Google, and Perplexity. Neither Sierra nor Meta tied the protocol's launch date to that incident, but the sequence is hard to ignore: an agent that scrapes a site pretending to be a human user gets banned; three weeks later, the same company co-announces a standard built specifically to let agents identify themselves, get explicit permission, and avoid exactly that confrontation. Reading PAP as Meta's attempt to build a legitimate front door after getting caught at the back one is an interpretation, not something either company stated, but it is the most direct explanation the public record supports.

There is a second, less personal pressure: PAP is not the only standard in this fight. A comparison in Beri's analysis lists the Universal Commerce Protocol (Google, Amazon, Shopify, Microsoft, Stripe) for discovery and checkout, the Agentic Commerce Protocol (OpenAI, Stripe) for in-agent payment, and the Trusted Agent Protocol (Visa, Cloudflare) for agent identity. Shopify and Stripe are backing more than one of these simultaneously. For Meta, sitting out while rivals set the terms of agentic commerce was the less attractive option.

## Who It Affects

Amazon is the most conspicuous absence from the partner list, and it is already the most restrictive of the major platforms toward outside agents; a coalition forming around an alternative standard makes its walled-garden posture more visible by contrast. OpenAI and Anthropic are also missing. Taylor told [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/06/meta-teams-up-with-bret-taylors-sierra-technologies-on-new-standards-for-ai-agent-commerce/) he expects OpenAI to eventually participate and would be "really disappointed" if competitors didn't, but OpenAI already co-owns a competing standard with Stripe, which gives it little reason to adopt a protocol it didn't help write. For merchants and payment companies, the near-term effect is pressure to support multiple, overlapping standards at once rather than wait for one winner.

## What to Watch Next

Three concrete signals will show whether this is infrastructure or a press release. First, whether the version 0.1 specification due later this month ships with real licensing and governance terms, not just technical documentation, addressing the gap Beri's analysis identifies. Second, whether OpenAI, Anthropic, Amazon, or Google ever join as named partners, rather than being described only as companies Taylor hopes will participate. Third, and most concretely, whether a non-Meta agent can complete an authorized transaction with a non-Sierra business without going through a registry either company controls, the interoperability test Beri proposes checking by January 2027. Until one of those three resolves, the Personal Agent Protocol is a credible proposal backed by real commercial partners, not yet a standard anyone is bound to.
