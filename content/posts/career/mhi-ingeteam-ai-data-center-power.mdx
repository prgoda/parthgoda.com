## What Was Actually Agreed

On October 15, [Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI) and the Spanish electrical equipment maker Ingeteam announced a memorandum of understanding](https://www.mhi.com/news/26101502.html) to distribute, integrate, and jointly develop energy infrastructure for US data centers. Stripped of the framing, it is a distribution deal: Ingeteam's power conversion hardware and EMS/SCADA control software — the systems that manage how electricity actually behaves once it reaches a facility — will be sold into the US market through MHI's existing data center distribution and integration channels, built around the Dallas-based business MHI stood up in 2025 and the US power systems firm Concentric it acquired in 2023. The companies also said they'll evaluate joint development of next-generation architectures, including medium-voltage distribution technology, though that work is explicitly subject to further technical and commercial review. It's a non-binding MOU: no financial terms, contract duration, exclusivity, or named customers were disclosed in the announcement or in the trade coverage that followed, and none of the reporting found suggests a signed, binding agreement yet exists.

## Why Now

The pressure is specific: AI training and inference create dynamic, spiky load profiles that cause harmonic distortion and voltage imbalance inside a data center, which shortens equipment life and threatens uptime. That's a different problem from simply not having enough megawatts, and it's the one MHI's Shin Gomi, senior general manager of its data center and energy management department, pointed to directly: "The challenge is no longer just securing power. It is delivering high-quality, reliable power to support AI workloads." Grid interconnection queues have also been lengthening nationally, pushing operators to treat power quality and grid coordination as design problems rather than afterthoughts. Ingeteam brings five decades of power-electronics and grid-stability work, mostly from wind and utility-scale solar — including delivering more than 1 gigawatt of solar PV conversion systems to Acciona Energía in Texas — but it has no established US data center sales track record of its own. MHI has the US distribution relationships and data-center ambitions but, by its own framing, needed deeper power-conversion and medium-voltage expertise to credibly sell a "power quality" story rather than just hardware.

## Who It Affects

The US data center power market, estimated around $35 billion and projected toward $50 billion by 2030, is already held by Vertiv, Schneider Electric, Eaton, ABB, and Siemens, several of which have been acquiring their way into adjacent categories like liquid cooling. MHI and Ingeteam are positioning as challengers in power quality and grid interface specifically, not the broader power and cooling stack. Data center operators get another vendor option; incumbents get a new combined competitor that didn't exist as a unit on October 14.

## What to Watch Next

Three things would turn this from positioning into substance: a signed, binding agreement rather than a renewable MOU; a named pilot customer or data center operator; and disclosed commercial terms. One analyst note framed named partnerships like this one as historically preceding, rather than constituting, revenue events — worth testing against what actually follows.
