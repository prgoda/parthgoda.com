## What Was Actually Agreed

On October 26, [Rapidus and HCLTech announced a partnership](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/rapidus-and-hcltech-announce-partnership-for-next-generation-semiconductor-technologies-302919222.html) and opened two HCLTech-run design centers, in Bengaluru and Hubli, dedicated to Rapidus's chip engineering. The centers will handle mask design, design infrastructure, process design kit development, design technology co-optimization, and layout design — the detailed work that sits between a target chip and a working wafer at Rapidus's planned 2nm fab in Hokkaido. Beyond the two sites, the companies also agreed to build a longer-term pipeline of Indian engineers for Rapidus's workforce needs in Japan. Neither company disclosed what the deal is worth, how many engineers are involved, or how long it runs.

As the diagram shows, Rapidus is handing off a layer of its core engineering work to a partner it doesn't control, in exchange for access to engineers it can't easily find at home. HCLTech gets its second named chipmaker client in a year and a half, after joining [Samsung's Advanced Foundry Ecosystem as a design-solution partner](https://www.hcltech.com/en-us/press-releases/hcltech-joins-samsung-advanced-foundry-ecosystem-design-solution-partner) in March 2025 — evidence it's building an actual semiconductor-engineering line of business, not an IT-services sideline. What HCLTech gives up is less tangible: its name is now publicly attached to whether a chronically delayed, government-subsidized fab ever ships.

Rapidus was founded in 2022 to revive Japan's advanced chipmaking, with government backing that has since grown past $6 billion and a licensing arrangement with IBM for the underlying 2nm process. Its timeline has already slipped once — a pilot line meant for 2025 moved into 2026 — which makes 2027 mass production a deadline the project can't afford to miss again, especially after a ¥267.6 billion ($1.7 billion) funding round in February pulled in 32 private backers betting it gets there. Japan's engineering labor market is tight and aging, and India trains far more VLSI-trained engineers than its own chip industry currently employs. HCLTech CEO C. Vijayakumar framed the timing plainly in the companies' announcement: "Advanced semiconductors will define the technologies of the next decade."

## Who It Affects

HCLTech's domestic rivals are making the same bet on the same labor pool. TCS has built out chiplet design and packaging services, and Infosys acquired the design-services firm InSemi — both chasing the same Indian engineers and the same class of foundry clients. Samsung, HCLTech's other foundry partner, now shares its design-solution partner with a competing national chip project. And Rapidus's backers — Toyota, Sony, NTT, SoftBank, and the Japanese taxpayers funding most of this — are now counting on an outside partner's hiring to help hit a deadline the company has already missed once.

## What to Watch Next

The real test isn't the ribbon-cutting in Bengaluru, it's whether Rapidus reaches 2nm volume production in 2027 with HCLTech's design work inside it. Watch for whether either company discloses deal size or headcount targets, whether HCLTech lands a third foundry client alongside Samsung and Rapidus, and whether Rapidus's next funding or schedule update treats this partnership as a sign it's catching up — or as one more dependency on the way to a deadline it has already pushed once.
