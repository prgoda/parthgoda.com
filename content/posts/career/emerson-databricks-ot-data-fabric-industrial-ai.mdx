## What Was Actually Agreed

On November 19, [Emerson announced](https://www.emerson.com/en/corporate/news/2026/emerson-databricks-bring-industrial-and-enterprise-ai-together) that its AspenTech business is collaborating with Databricks so that the AspenTech Inmation OT Data Fabric gives native access, from inside the Databricks Data + AI Platform, to contextualized operational data pulled straight from plant equipment, sensors, and historians — data that previously needed custom integration work to reach a general-purpose analytics platform. The deal also opens a path from Databricks into AspenTech AVA, the industrial decision-support AI layer Emerson launched in May. Databricks' manufacturing industry lead, Shiv Trisal, said the goal is to help organizations "democratize access to data and AI." Neither company disclosed financial terms or exclusivity, and trade coverage from [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/emerson-and-databricks-collaborate-to-bring-industrial-and-enterprise-ai-together/) notes that native customer access is still forthcoming rather than live today.

## Why Now

Emerson is the third major automation vendor to plug its operational data into Databricks rather than build a competing platform, and the diagram above shows the pattern isn't new — it's accelerating. AVEVA struck the original Databricks tie-up in 2024 and shipped it in 2025; Siemens followed in June 2026, routing Industrial Edge data into Databricks through a third-party connector. Manufacturers are under pressure to apply the same large language models reshaping their back offices to decades of siloed sensor data, and none of the plant-floor incumbents wants to be the one still asking customers to build that pipeline by hand.

## What Each Side Gets

Databricks gets a third automation giant routing OT data through its lakehouse instead of a rival's, deepening its position as the default place enterprises build AI on top of plant data regardless of whose control system produced it. Emerson gets to put Inmation and AVA in front of every customer already standardized on Databricks for IT-side analytics, without having to win a separate data-platform sale of its own. Reading Emerson as following AVEVA and Siemens rather than leading them is an interpretation — none of the three companies frames it as a response to the others — but the sequence of announcements is public record.

## Who It Affects

Rockwell Automation, the other dominant player in industrial automation, took a different path: a deepened, multi-year partnership with Microsoft built on Azure IoT Operations. Manufacturers running Rockwell control systems now face a real choice about which AI data layer absorbs their plant data, since that choice will shape whose AI tooling becomes the default lens on their operations. AVEVA, notably, also runs a separate [collaboration with Snowflake](https://www.aveva.com/en/perspectives/blog/ai-ready-industrial-data-with-aveva-and-snowflake/) on AI-ready industrial data architectures — hedging across lakehouses much as Shopify and Stripe hedge across competing agentic-commerce standards elsewhere in enterprise tech. Honeywell, the remaining major incumbent, is absent from all of this; none of the sources reviewed for this piece describe a comparable OT-to-AI-platform deal on its part.

## What to Watch Next

Three signals will separate real adoption from a logo swap: whether Emerson discloses a date when native customer access to the integration actually goes live; whether Honeywell picks a side or builds its own path; and whether Rockwell's Microsoft-anchored customers face enough pressure from peers running Databricks-based AI to request a similar bridge.
